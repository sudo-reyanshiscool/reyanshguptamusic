The story of the cover

The cover of First Light isn't a picture of a sunrise. It's the piece itself: every note I wrote, drawn once around a circle.

It started as a sunrise

The first version was the obvious one: a sun coming up over water, with five thin lines across the sky like a musical staff. It was pleasant, and I didn't like it. I wanted a cover people would stare at for a solid fifteen minutes, and nobody stares at a sunrise they've seen before.

Drawing the music instead

The second idea was to stop illustrating the title and draw the composition. I wrote First Light in MuseScore, so the score already existed as data. Exported as MIDI, it becomes a list of every note in the piece: when it starts, how long it lasts, how high it is, how loud it is, and which of the fourteen instruments plays it.

That list has about ten thousand entries for five minutes of music, because every stroke of a timpani roll or a string tremolo counts as its own note. Each entry became one small arc of light.

How to read it

  • Time runs clockwise from the top. One lap of the circle is the whole piece, and the minute marks sit around the rim.
  • Pitch runs outward. The double basses sit near the centre in violet; the flutes and violins reach the edge in gold.
  • Loudness is light. A quiet note is a faint thread. A loud one is thick and bright, and throws a ray out past the edge.
  • Timpani are the thin spokes fanning out from the centre, one for every stroke.

Building it up

The picture is made in layers, and every layer is the same ten thousand notes drawn a different way.

What you can see in it

Because nothing on the cover is decoration, you can read the shape of the piece straight off it. The top right is the opening, marked Adagio, misterioso: a few long, faint arcs with a lot of dark between them.

The blaze at the bottom is the fast section, where the tempo jumps to 138 beats a minute and the whole orchestra is playing. The stepped lines there are scales running up and down. On the left, around the four-minute mark, the gold cluster is the last loud passage. After that the arcs thin out and the ring fades back into the dark where it began.

The lettering

I tried seven typefaces on the finished artwork, from thin classical serifs to wide modern ones. The elegant options looked like every other orchestral score. The one that worked was the heaviest and widest, because it's the only thing on the page solid enough to stand next to all that light. The tempo marks around the rim are set small, in italics, the way they'd be written in the score.

How it was made

Nothing on the cover was painted, photographed or made with an image generator. The picture is drawn by a short program that reads the notes and plots them, which means it is exact: change one note in the score and the cover changes with it.
